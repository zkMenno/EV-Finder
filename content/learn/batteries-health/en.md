# Batteries and used battery health

The battery is the most expensive part of an electric car, and most batteries age slowly. This page explains how batteries wear, what "battery health" means, and what to check before you buy a used car. For the different [[chemistry|battery types]], see the page "Battery types explained".

## How fast do batteries wear?

Slowly, for most cars. Some numbers from large studies:

- **About 2 % a year.** Geotab looked at more than 22,700 electric vehicles in 2025. On average they lost **2.3 %** of their capacity a year. At that rate a battery still has about **82 %** after eight years ([Geotab](https://www.geotab.com/ie/press-release/ev-battery-health-degradation-fast-charging-study/)). Cars that used fast chargers for under 12 % of their charging lost about 1.5 % a year; cars that used them more lost about 2.5 % ([Geotab](https://www.geotab.com/fleet-management-solutions/ev-battery-degradation-tool/)). These were fleet cars, many in North America.
- **Still above 90 % after many kilometres.** The battery testing company Aviloo looked at more than 500,000 of its own tests on used cars of 20 popular models, from 2022 to 2026. Even at 150,000 km, the typical car of most models still had more than 90 % ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/806798/ev-battery-aging-study-2026/)).
- **A real long-term test.** The German motoring club ADAC drove a Volkswagen ID.3 more than 160,000 km in four years. The battery still had 91 % of its usable capacity at the end ([Volkswagen](https://www.volkswagen-newsroom.com/en/press-releases/over-90-per-cent-capacity-id3-battery-impresses-in-adacs-160000-kilometre-endurance-test-19433)).
- **Few batteries are replaced.** In a 2024 study of more than 20,000 cars by Recurrent, about 2.5 % had a new battery. For cars built in 2016 or later it was 1 % or less; most replacements were on the very first cars, built before 2015 ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/720398/study-battery-replacement-rates-evs/)).

A battery loses a little more in its first years, then the loss slows down. It rarely stops working all at once.

## Averages hide big differences

Two cars of the same model, age and mileage can be quite different. In the Aviloo data, battery health of the same model differed by up to **13.5 %** between cars ([Autotalk](https://autotalk.com.au/industry-news/ev-battery-health-can-vary-by-more-than-13-study-finds)), and the spread grows as the kilometres go up ([newmobility.news](https://newmobility.news/2026/09/03/ev-batteries-age-better-than-feared-500000-tests-show/)). Note that Aviloo sells battery tests, so it has an interest in this message. That is why you check the car itself, not just the model.

## What "battery health" means

[[soh|Battery health]] (state of health, SoH) compares how much energy the battery can hold today with how much it held when new. 90 % means about 10 % less range than new.

Different tools measure it in different ways, so two reports can differ by a few points. A small difference is not a warning sign; a low number is.

## What makes a battery age faster

- **Heat.** Hot climates and long, hot fast-charging sessions add wear. Geotab found cars that spent a lot of time in heat aged a little faster ([Geotab](https://www.geotab.com/fleet-management-solutions/ev-battery-degradation-tool/)).
- **No active [[cooling|battery cooling]].** Some older cars, such as the first Nissan Leaf, have no liquid cooling. Their batteries heat up more and often aged faster. On these cars, the health check matters even more.
- **Lots of [[dc-charging|fast charging]].** It is fine to use, but a car that lived on fast chargers often shows more wear (see the Geotab numbers above).
- **Sitting full or empty for long.** For most [[nmc|nickel-based (NMC)]] batteries, makers advise about 80–90 % for daily use. [[lfp|LFP]] batteries are the exception: most makers say [[charge-to-100|charging to 100 %]] is fine. Always follow the car's manual.

## Warranty: what is left?

Most makers guarantee a minimum battery health for a set time. A common promise is **70 % after 8 years or 160,000 km**, whichever comes first; Volkswagen gives exactly that for its ID. cars ([Volkswagen](https://www.vw.ca/en/electric-vehicles/about-electric-vehicles/battery-tech-and-maintenance.html)). Terms differ per maker and sometimes per country. On a used car, work out how many years and kilometres are left, and ask whether the warranty passes to you.

## Belgium: battery health on the Car-Pass

In Belgium, the Car-Pass (the mileage record a seller must give you) can show the battery's health since 2026 ([Car-Pass](https://www.car-pass.be/en/blog/soh-van-een-batterij-bij-elektrische-tweedehandsautos)). A certified tester measures it and sends the result to Car-Pass ([newmobility.news](https://newmobility.news/en/2025/10/24/belgiums-car-pass-pilots-battery-health-checks-before-eu-takes-over/)). For now it is voluntary; one report says it becomes compulsory in February 2027 ([newmobility.news](https://newmobility.news/en/2026/08/26/dieteren-second-hand-evs-retain-95-9-battery-capacity-on-average/)). In other countries, ask the seller for a recent independent battery test.

## Checklist for a used electric car

1. **Ask for a recent battery health report** from an independent test or a dealer readout. Be wary if the seller won't share one.
2. **Compare it with the model.** Similar cars of the same age and mileage give you a reference.
3. **Check the warranty left:** years, kilometres and the minimum health it promises.
4. **Ask how it was charged:** mostly at home, or mostly fast charging? Long periods at 100 % (for NMC) or in heat?
5. **Check the cooling** on older or cheap models: air-cooled packs need a closer look.
6. **Test-drive with a fairly full battery** and look at the range the car shows, knowing that it depends on temperature and driving.

A good battery report can also help on price: battery health is one of the things that moves the [[used-price|used price]].
