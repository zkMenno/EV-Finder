# Winter range explained

Electric cars go less far in the cold. That is normal, and it is not a sign of a broken battery. This page explains how much range you lose, why, and what helps.

## How much range do you lose?

It depends on the car, the temperature and how you drive. Some numbers from large studies and tests:

- **Around freezing (0 °C):** in a study of more than 18,000 cars by Recurrent, the average car kept about **80 %** of its range ([Kelley Blue Book](https://www.kbb.com/car-news/study-the-average-ev-keeps-80-of-its-range-in-the-cold)). The cars were in the US, compared with their range in mild weather.
- **With or without a heat pump:** in the same study, cars with a heat pump kept about **83 %** on average, cars without one about **75 %** ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/747374/ev-range-loss-cold-heat-pump-data/)).
- **Big differences between models:** the results ran from 63 % to 89 % of normal range kept, depending on the model ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/747374/ev-range-loss-cold-heat-pump-data/)).
- **A cold Norwegian winter day:** in the El Prix test 2025 by the Norwegian motoring club NAF, below freezing, 18 of the 24 new cars ended up 11 % to 23 % short of their [[wltp|official range]] ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/747548/ev-winter-range-test-norway-2025/)).
- **Deep frost:** in the El Prix winter test 2026, at temperatures down to −32 °C, the 24 cars fell on average **38 %** short of their official range ([GreenFleet](https://www.greenfleet.net/node/20095)).

So on a normal winter day, plan for clearly less range than in summer. In a hard frost, or on a fast motorway, the loss can be much bigger.

## Why the range drops

Several things add up:

- **Heating the cabin uses battery power.** A petrol car heats with waste heat from its engine. An electric car has very little waste heat, so it uses battery energy to keep you warm.
- **A cold battery works less well.** Cold slows the chemistry inside the cells, so the battery gives less and takes charge more slowly.
- **Winter driving itself costs more:** winter tyres, wet roads and cold, dense air all add resistance.

Heating costs energy per hour, not per kilometre. That's why short, slow trips in town lose the most range in percentage terms: you spend a lot of time heating for few kilometres. On a motorway, speed matters more.

## Speed matters a lot

Driving faster takes much more energy, in any season. In one test by Bjørn Nyland, a Volkswagen ID.3 used 135 Wh/km at 90 km/h and 205 Wh/km at 120 km/h: about half as much again. Its range fell from 413 km to 271 km ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/445471/vw-id3-range-test-range-efficiency/)). Other cars differ, but the direction is always the same. In winter, slowing down a bit on the motorway is one of the easiest ways to get more range.

## What helps

- **A [[heat-pump|heat pump]].** It heats the cabin with less energy than a simple electric heater. On many used cars it was an option, so check whether the car you look at has one.
- **Warm up while still plugged in.** Most electric cars let you heat the cabin (and often the battery) before you leave, using power from the charger instead of from the battery. Use the app or a departure timer.
- **[[preconditioning|Warm the battery before fast charging]].** A cold battery charges slowly. Many cars warm it up when you navigate to a fast charger. In the El Prix 2026 test, many cars came close to their stated 10–80 % charging time after preconditioning, even in deep frost ([GreenFleet](https://www.greenfleet.net/node/20095)).
- **Seat and steering wheel heating.** Heating your body directly uses less energy than heating all the air in the car.
- **Park inside if you can.** A battery that starts warmer loses less.

## The battery type plays a role

Some [[chemistry|battery types]] cope with cold better than others. [[lfp|LFP]] batteries are known for losing more range and charging speed in the cold than nickel-based batteries, unless they are warmed up first. See our page on battery chemistries.
