# Battery types (chemistries) explained

Every electric car battery is made of cells, and the materials inside those cells are called the [[chemistry|chemistry]]. It affects range, price, how the car copes with cold, and how full you should charge it every day.

**One rule first:** the car's own manual always wins. The advice below is general guidance that experts agree on, not a lab test of your car. Makers often don't say which chemistry a car uses; then we show "[[not-disclosed|not disclosed]]" rather than guess.

## The two big families

### Nickel-based: NMC and NCA

[[nmc|NMC]] (nickel manganese cobalt) and [[nca|NCA]] (nickel cobalt aluminium) are the most common types in European electric cars so far.

- **Good:** a lot of energy per kilo, so more range. They usually cope better with cold than LFP.
- **Watch out:** they age faster when they sit full or hot for a long time. They use nickel and cobalt, which are costly materials.
- **Looking after it:** most makers advise charging to about 80–90 % for daily use and to 100 % just before a long trip. Don't leave the car parked for days at 100 % or near empty.

### Iron phosphate: LFP

[[lfp|LFP]] (lithium iron phosphate) has grown fast, especially in cheaper cars.

- **Good:** long life, a good safety record, and cheaper to make: no nickel or cobalt. Most makers say [[charge-to-100|charging to 100 %]] every day is fine.
- **Watch out:** less energy for the same weight, so a bigger or heavier battery for the same range. In the cold, range and fast-charging speed drop more, unless the battery is warmed first. The car's estimate of how full the battery is can drift.
- **Looking after it:** charge to 100 % regularly (many makers say at least once a week) so the car can recalibrate. In winter, [[preconditioning|warm up the battery]] before fast charging.

## Older cars

### Manganese blend (LMO/NMC)

Several early electric cars, such as the first Nissan Leaf, used a [[lmo-nmc|manganese-based blend]]. Combined with no active [[cooling|battery cooling]], these packs could age fast in hot weather.

- **Buying used:** check the [[soh|battery health (state of health)]] before you buy. It matters more than age or mileage alone. Avoid many fast charges on hot days.

## New and coming types

These are new, so there is little long-term data from real cars. Treat range and lifetime claims with care.

- **[[lmfp|LMFP]]** (LFP with manganese added): aims for a bit more range than LFP with similar safety. Few cars use it yet. Follow the manual; LFP rules may not apply.
- **[[sodium-ion|Sodium-ion]]:** uses sodium instead of lithium, and no cobalt or nickel. In February 2026, CATL and Changan unveiled what they call the first mass-produced passenger car with a sodium-ion battery. CATL says the cells store up to 175 Wh/kg, close to LFP, and keep over 90 % of their capacity at −40 °C. The car was to go on sale in China in mid-2026 ([CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2026/02/05/catl-sodium-batteries-begin-deployment-in-production-passenger-vehicles/)). Those are maker claims; real-world data will take years.
- **[[semi-solid|Semi-solid-state]]:** part of the liquid inside the cell is replaced by a gel or solid. NIO started offering a 150 kWh semi-solid battery in 2023 ([electrive](https://www.electrive.com/2023/07/04/nio-has-started-integrating-150-kwh-semi-solid-state-batteries)), but stopped making it in 2025 after a few hundred units because of low demand. It won't come to Europe ([electrive](https://www.electrive.com/2025/11/17/nio-drops-150-kwh-battery-with-semi-solid-electrolyte-again/)). The name is used loosely in marketing.
- **[[all-solid-state|All-solid-state]]:** no liquid at all. It promises more range and faster charging, but it is not yet in cars you can buy. Toyota says its first car with one could come in 2027 or 2028 ([Electrek](https://electrek.co/2025/10/08/toyota-aims-to-launch-worlds-first-all-solid-state-ev-batteries/)). Treat announced figures as targets, not facts.
- **[[lmr|LMR]]** (lithium manganese-rich): aims for range like NMC with less cobalt and nickel. Not yet in series cars as far as we know.

## Cooling matters too

How the car keeps the battery at a good temperature matters as much as the chemistry. Liquid cooling is the most common. Air-cooled or uncooled packs heat up more during fast charging and in hot weather, and may age faster. On used cars this is worth checking. See [[cooling|battery cooling]].

## What this means for your choice

- Mostly short trips and you like to charge to full every night? LFP makes that easy.
- Long trips, or a cold climate? A nickel-based battery, or any car with a [[preconditioning|battery warm-up]] and a heat pump, helps. Cold-weather results differ a lot by model ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/747374/ev-range-loss-cold-heat-pump-data/)), so check each car's real-world figures.
- Buying used? The battery's health tells you more than the chemistry name.
